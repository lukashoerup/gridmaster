## 1. Power Grid (Funkenschlag) and its family: how it works, why it is a classic, what critics say, and what transfers

### Takeaway
Power Grid still rates about 7.8 on BGG (rank about #76, weight 3.3) twenty years after release. Its appeal comes from four interlocking systems:
- open auctions over a visible, sorted market of power plants;
- a shared fuel market: players drain its price ladder, and a schedule that depends on the Step and the player count refills it;
- a map network whose building and connection costs keep rising;
- a payout with diminishing returns, plus a turn order that penalises the leader.

Three "Steps", triggered by player progress, pace all of this. Critics consistently name three problems: the arithmetic load and analysis paralysis, determinism, and catch-up rules that feel "gamey" and reward sandbagging (deliberately staying behind). The official mobile app is poorly rated.

For a single-player tycoon with real-time-with-pause, these parts transfer:
- the price ladder that the players' own actions move;
- the window showing both the current and the upcoming plant market;
- Steps triggered by progress;
- built-in obsolescence;
- maps that work as rule modules;
- modular "robot" AI opponents;
- scoring what you power rather than what you build.

The turn-order catch-up and the "do the sums yourself" burden do not transfer.

### Cited Findings

**Origin and design intent**
- Friese started from wanting a crayon network game (inspired by *Dampfross* and *Empire Builder*) "but not drawing railways, just another network. The first idea was power lines and almost immediately, everything needed came in my mind: power plants, resources, resource market, cities, rivers, mountains on a map, money to earn." — [Opinionated Gamers, "Funkenschlag at Fifteen: The Story of Power Grid" (Chris Wray, 2016)](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/07/11/funkenschlag-at-fifteen-the-story-of-power-grid/); [Dice Tower Dish, "Friedemann Friese part 2"](https://dicetowerdish.com/2019/12/10/friedemann-friese-part-2-frieses-first-forays-into-fun/)
- The network-building costs were inspired by *Empire Builder*. The resource market came from the older economic games *McMulti* and *Playboss*. — [Opinionated Gamers 2016](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/07/11/funkenschlag-at-fifteen-the-story-of-power-grid/)
- The 2001 *Funkenschlag* was a crayon edition in which players drew their own networks. When ALEA showed interest, editor Stefan Brück "didn't believe in the crayons", so Friese made a version without them. The fixed-map Power Grid was first released in 2004. — [Opinionated Gamers 2016](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2016/07/11/funkenschlag-at-fifteen-the-story-of-power-grid/); [Wikipedia: Power Grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Grid)

**Round structure and core mechanisms**
- Each round has five phases: determine player order → auction power plants → buy resources → build → bureaucracy. — [BGG wiki, "How To Teach: Power Grid"](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Teach:_Power_Grid); [My Board Game Guides, rules overview](https://myboardgameguides.com/guides/how-to-play/power-grid-rules-overview)
- In the auction, the player in position 1 is the first auctioneer and picks a plant from the top row (the "current market"). The minimum opening bid is the number printed at the plant card's upper left. — [BGG "How To Teach"](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Teach:_Power_Grid)
- Plant market: until Step 3, the highest-numbered plant is taken away each round. In Step 3 only six plants are offered, all available at once, with no "future market" row. — [RPG.net review of Power Grid Recharged](https://www.rpg.net/reviews/archive/18/18253.phtml); [BGG "How To Teach"](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Teach:_Power_Grid) (search extract; exact page attribution approximate)
- In the bureaucracy phase, each player says how many cities they will supply with electricity and is "paid according to the schedule" (the payment table). — [officialgamerules.org](https://officialgamerules.org/game-rules/power-grid/); [UltraBoardGames rules](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/power-grid/game-rules.php)
- Resupply: in the bureaucracy phase the resource market is refilled from a table indexed by player count and by Step (1/2/3), separately for each of the four resources (coal, oil, garbage, uranium). The refill fills "starting from the highest value space". — [BGG "How To Teach"](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Teach:_Power_Grid); [Power Grid rules PDF (tesera.ru mirror)](https://tesera.ru/images/items/11611/Power_Grid_Rules_EN.pdf); [world-of-board-games rules PDF](https://world-of-board-games.com.sg/docs/PowerGrid.pdf)
- Each map can carry its own resource-supply tables: the Québec / Baden-Württemberg expansion rules contain a separate supply table for each map. — [Rio Grande Games, Québec/Baden-Württemberg rules PDF](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Power-Grid-Quebec-Rules.pdf)

**Catch-up and turn order**
- Zatu summarises the turn order as "the player with the largest network goes last, while the player in last place goes first". It adds that "going later has advantages—especially when buying resources—but push too far ahead and you risk paying inflated prices for fuel, while hanging back too long means someone else might sprint ahead before you can react." — [Zatu, "Power Grid second opinion: where calculators are the real power plants"](https://zatu.com/en-au/blogs/reviews/power-grid-second-opinion-where-calculators-are-the-real-power-plants); [Zatu, Power Grid review](https://zatu.com/en-us/blogs/reviews/power-grid-review)
- "Some players quietly stay behind for most of the match, only to leap forward in a single explosive turn and win". This sandbagging is treated as a legitimate tactic. — [Zatu second opinion](https://zatu.com/en-au/blogs/reviews/power-grid-second-opinion-where-calculators-are-the-real-power-plants); [My Board Game Guides, strategy dos and don'ts](https://myboardgameguides.com/game-strategy/game-specific-strategy/power-grid-strategy-tips-dos-and-donts/)
- "Catch the leader" is listed as a core mechanism. German-language commentary calls the game's architecture "mathematically brilliant" at preventing a runaway leader. — [Wikipedia: Power Grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Grid); [analog.games (DE)](https://analog.games/games/5a5772ef-17e6-47a0-babb-a554a6ce1a10?lang=de)
- A German board-game podcast episode about the game is titled "Funkenschlag – die Erfolgsangst" ("the fear of success"). — [podcast.de](https://www.podcast.de/episode/701263422/015-funkenschlag-die-erfolgsangst)

**Steps and end condition**
- Step 2 begins once a player has built a target number of cities: 7 with 5 or fewer players, 10 in a 2-player game, 6 with 6 players. Reaching the target does not flip the Step immediately; the change follows the timing rules. — [edp.org rules PDF](https://edp.org/Games/PowerGrid.pdf); [BGG Power Grid FAQ](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/Power_Grid_FAQ); [UltraBoardGames](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/power-grid/game-rules.php)
- The game ends at the end of the round in which a player builds 21 cities (2 players), 17 (3–4 players) or 15 (5–6 players). (See Gaps: the 6-player figure may be 14.) — [UltraBoardGames beginner's guide](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/power-grid/beginners-guide.php); [officialgamerules.org](https://officialgamerules.org/game-rules/power-grid/)

**Criticisms**
- **Arithmetic.** "You'll spend most of your time in Power Grid doing computation, budgeting out three phases of spending each round and readjusting this budget with every dollar spent and each speculation made". It is "one of the few games that allows calculators at the table, especially towards endgame". The review titles carry the same message: "Turning Electrical Infrastructure Into High-Stakes Speculation" and "where calculators are the real power plants". — [Roll to Review](https://rolltoreview.com/?p=86); [Zatu second opinion](https://zatu.com/en-au/blogs/reviews/power-grid-second-opinion-where-calculators-are-the-real-power-plants)
- **Analysis paralysis.** Players are left feeling "that a couple more minutes would allow you to compute a better move", and players prone to analysis paralysis are advised to avoid the game. — [Wikipedia: Power Grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Grid); [analog.games](https://analog.games/games/5a5772ef-17e6-47a0-babb-a554a6ce1a10?lang=en)
- **Determinism and "gamey" balancing.** "If you know the turn order, you can see exactly what's going to happen, so strategy boils down to doing a long series of fairly simple sums". The weakest-player-goes-first rule and the diminishing income from bigger networks are called "massively gamey attempts to stop runaway leader problems". — [There Will Be Games, "Power Grid: A Calculated Assessment"](https://therewillbe.games/articles-boardgame-reviews/1874-power-grid-a-calculated-assessment); [Critical Video Game Studies, game-design notes](https://gamedesign.criticalvideogamestudies.com/?p=180) (the extract does not make clear which of these two pages each quote comes from)

### Inferences
1. **The resource market is the most transferable device, but in inverted form.** In Power Grid, fuel cost is a function of everyone's consumption. Your purchase visibly raises the price for whoever buys after you, and the designer tunes scarcity through the refill rate per Step and per map.
   - Gridmaster's equivalent is the merit-order supply stack in each market and hour. Renewables enter at near-zero marginal cost and push the clearing price *down* for everyone, including the player's existing fleet.
   - Power Grid players are fuel buyers hurt by scarcity. A renewables player is a price-taker hurt by abundance. Both need the same thing: the ladder made visible, with the player's own footprint on it.
2. **The two-row plant market is a forecasting window.** Players see the next four plants, so a purchase is a plan rather than a lottery. Gridmaster needs an equivalent "future market": rivals' announced projects, the grid-connection queue, scheduled tenders and technology cost curves. That way price cannibalisation, where adding more solar lowers the price all solar earns, can be anticipated rather than ambushing the player.
3. **Steps triggered by progress are the best pacing idea in the box.** Step 2 is triggered by someone reaching 7 cities, so the game's phase follows the state of play, not the clock. Gridmaster can tie market regime changes to penetration thresholds, alongside its historical calendar. This fits Gridmaster's own premise that batteries arrive "as price volatility rises".
4. **Diminishing returns are the right anti-snowball tool, but they should be thematic.** Critics call Power Grid's concave payout "gamey" because it is a designer's table. Gridmaster can get the same concavity from real causes, so it reads as simulation rather than a thumb on the scale:
   - merit-order cannibalisation;
   - depletion of the best sites;
   - grid congestion and curtailment.
5. **Turn-order catch-up does not translate to single-player, and its side-effect is a warning.** Sandbagging, the "fear of success", is exactly what a tycoon must not reward: deliberate underperformance must never be optimal. If rubber-banding is needed, deliver it as opportunity (distressed assets, cheaper late technology), never as a penalty for leading.
6. **"Cities powered, not cities built."** Power Grid scores the cities you can actually supply, which takes plants, fuel and network together. The equivalent rule for Gridmaster: revenue and score come from delivered MWh × capture price (the average price a technology actually earns when it produces). Nameplate capacity alone earns nothing, apart from any explicit capacity payments.
7. **Obsolescence through the market keeps choices fresh.** Plants are culled, and Step 3 reshapes the market. Gridmaster should retire technology tiers and support schemes on a schedule the player can see coming.
8. **Do the maths for the player, but keep uncertainty.** Power Grid's calculators and analysis paralysis come from perfect information plus manual arithmetic. A video game can automate settlement and show projections. If it also stays fully deterministic, it risks feeling like a solved spreadsheet, so the challenge should come from uncertainty: weather years, rival behaviour and policy.
9. **Map packs as rule modules are proven**, both commercially (16+ maps) and as teaching: France's nuclear, Poland's coal, Benelux's eco plants, nuclear politics in Central Europe. This is a direct precedent for Gridmaster's country modifiers and for a possible DLC model.
10. **The Robots' five behaviour tiles are a ready template** for single-player AI competitors: initial siting, bidding, input purchasing, expansion and a special ability.
11. **The weak app reception (2.5★) suggests that porting a math-heavy board-game interface does not delight digital players.** Borrow the system dynamics, not the phase-by-phase interface.

### Gaps
- Exact rule numbers could not be fetched (BGG and the rule PDFs were blocked). The following are **unverified** (from background knowledge) and should be checked against the [Rio Grande Deluxe rules PDF](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Power-Grid-Deluxe-Rules.pdf) or the original rules before use:
  - **Payment table.** For 0–20 cities powered: 10, 22, 33, 44, 54, 64, 73, 82, 90, 98, 105, 112, 118, 124, 129, 134, 138, 142, 145, 148, 150 Elektro. The marginal income per extra city falls steadily from +12 to +2. This is the concave payout the critics discuss.
  - **Resource track.** Coal, oil and garbage have 3 units per price step 1–8. Uranium has single units at 1–8, then 10, 12, 14 and 16. Buyers take the cheapest units first, and refill fills the most expensive empty spaces first.
  - **City slots.** Each city has 3 slots: only the 10-Elektro slot in Step 1, the 15-Elektro slot from Step 2, the 20-Elektro slot from Step 3. Connection costs are printed on the map links.
  - **Plant market:**
    - 8 face-up plants sorted ascending: the top 4 are buyable, the bottom 4 are the future market.
    - A player can win at most one plant per round.
    - If nobody buys a plant in a round, the lowest plant is discarded.
    - Any plant numbered at or below the largest network's city count is discarded.
    - Each player holds at most 3 plants (4 in the 2-player game).
    - A plant stores up to twice its fuel input.
  - **Step 3.** A "Step 3" card sits under the plant deck. When it is drawn, the market becomes 6 plants, all buyable, and thereafter the smallest plant is removed instead of the largest.
  - **Turn order.**
    - The player with the most cities is position 1, with ties broken by the highest plant number, so the leader opens the auctions.
    - Buying resources and building run in reverse order, so the leader buys and builds last.
    - In round 1, turn order is re-sorted after the auction.
  - **Winner.** Most cities actually powered in the final bureaucracy; ties are broken by money.
  - **End threshold.** The 6-player figure is probably 14 (5 players: 15), against the search extract's "15 for 5–6".
  - **No emissions.** The base game has no emissions or pollution mechanism. Its "eco" plants (wind, solar, fusion) simply need no fuel.

## 2. Other energy, climate and industrial-economy board games: mechanics, how they model technology, emissions, markets and the transition, reception, and transferable ideas

### Takeaway
The best adjacent designs each isolate one lever Gridmaster needs:
- **CO2**: emission permits as a traded market, plus a shared pollution threshold.
- **Barrage**: water as a flowing shared resource, and capital locked up during construction.
- **Energy Empire** and **GigaWatt**: dice as an abstraction of variable sun and wind.
- **GigaWatt**: a race between European regions that includes resident protests.
- **Daybreak**: legible tracks, an optimistic framing and links to real-world explainers.
- **Keep Cool**: the choice between "black" and "green" growth.
- **Watts the Deal?**: household-scale balancing with batteries and trading.
- **Brass**: shared industrial networks and price-tracked markets.
- **18xx**: obsolescence ("rusting") that players can see coming, and phase changes triggered by buying new technology.

None of the games found models price cannibalisation, negative prices or storage arbitrage.

### Cited Findings

**CO2 (2012) and CO2: Second Chance (2018), Vital Lacerda**
- The game is set in the 1970s. Each player is the CEO of an energy company responding to government requests for new green power plants. The goal is to stop pollution rising while meeting the rising demand for sustainable energy, and to profit from doing so. — [Miniature Market listing](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/CO2-Second-Chance-2nd-Printing/MFG5000); [Board Game Bliss, CO2](https://www.boardgamebliss.com/collections/2-players/products/co)
- Mechanism tags: open drafting, semi-cooperative play, solo mode, area majority/influence and worker placement. Victory points come from money, Carbon Emission Permits (CEPs), green plants built, UN goals, company goals and "expertise" (the company's reputation). — [Board Game Bliss](https://www.boardgamebliss.com/collections/2-players/products/co)
- **Permits (CEPs).** Each region starts with CEPs granted by the UN. They must be spent to install the energy infrastructure for a project or to build a fossil-fuel plant. CEPs "can be bought and sold on a market, and their price fluctuates throughout the game". Installation costs a CEP. Energy summits "promote global awareness" and let companies share and gain expertise. — [UltraBoardGames, CO2 rules](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/co2/game-rules.php); [CO2 rules PDF](https://download.svet-deskovych-her.cz/rules/co2_EN.pdf); [Opinionated Gamers, Ben McJunkin review of CO2 (2013)](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2013/01/15/ben-mcjunkin-review-of-co2/)
- **Shared pollution track.** If global CO2 rises above 500 ppm, the game ends immediately in a shared loss. At 350 ppm or more a disaster strikes a region. Below 350 ppm, nothing happens. — [UltraBoardGames, CO2 rules](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/co2/game-rules.php)
- **Second Chance changes.** New iconography and rulebook, "more balanced gameplay", a new events deck, streamlined mechanisms, wooden plants instead of cardboard, a shorter play time, a co-operative mode and a goal-based solo mode. 1–4 players, 60–120 minutes, ages 12+. — [Miniature Market](https://www.miniaturemarket.com/CO2-Second-Chance-2nd-Printing/MFG5000); [Board Game Bliss, Second Chance](https://www.boardgamebliss.com/collections/2-players/products/co-second-chance)
- Reviews include "CO2: Second Chance Taught me Humanity is Doomed" ([There Will Be Games](https://therewillbe.games/articles-boardgame-reviews/8628-co2-second-chance-taught-me-humanity-is-doomed)), [Tabletop Gaming](https://www.tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/co2-second-chance-review/) and [Co-op Board Games](https://coopboardgames.com/cooperative-board-game-reviews/co%E2%82%82-second-chance-review/). Their content beyond the titles was not retrievable.

**The Manhattan Project: Energy Empire (Tom Jolly and Luke Laurie)** (likely the game meant by "Energy Empire")
- 1–5 players, about 30 minutes per player. Players are countries developing industry and energy production from roughly the end of World War II to the present, "constructing buildings and power plants while managing pollution in their environment". — [Board Game Quest](https://boardgamequest.com/manhattan-project-energy-empire-review); [Tabletop Gaming](https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/the-manhattan-project-energy-empire-review)
- It combines worker placement, tableau-building and resource management. Each turn a player either works or generates energy by rolling "energy dice" representing nuclear, coal, oil, solar and other sources. "No spaces are ever completely blocked since players may spend energy to use an occupied space". — [Board Game Quest](https://boardgamequest.com/manhattan-project-energy-empire-review)
- Praised as "a perfect combination of fulfilling, meaningful gameplay and rich, engrossing theme… made easy to understand by near-flawless design execution". — [Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/reviews/the-manhattan-project-energy-empire-review-tom-jolly-luke-laurie/); [Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/energy-empire-review) (attribution approximate). A "new version" is listed by [TLAMA games](https://www.tlamagames.com/en/board-games/manhattan-project--energy-empire--new-version/).

**Daybreak (2023), Matt Leacock and Matteo Menapace**
- A co-operative climate-action game. Each player controls a world power and deploys policies and technologies "to both dismantle the engine of global heating and to build resilient societies that protect people from life-threatening crises". — [Tabletop Gaming review](https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/daybreak-game-review); [Zatu review](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/daybreak-review)
- The blocs are China, Europe, the United States and the "Majority World". Each has a board that tracks, with tokens, its energy output, emissions, resilience (social, environmental, infrastructure) and crisis levels. — [Opinionated Gamers, Dale Yu review (Dec 2023)](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2023/12/08/dale-yu-review-of-daybreak/)
- Everyone loses if the global temperature gets too high or too many people in any bloc are in crisis. Everyone wins by drawing global emissions down to net zero. — [KCAW, "A new board game sparks hope about climate change"](https://www.kcaw.org/2023/12/23/a-new-board-game-sparks-hope-about-climate-change/); [Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/daybreak-review)
- "The primary drive here is engine-building", which feels counterintuitive in a co-op because of "a lack of player interaction". The teaching is "unobtrusive": QR codes on the cards link to explainers on policies such as universal public transport or microgrids. — [Tabletop Gaming](https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/daybreak-game-review)
- The tone is "bright, appealing design and upbeat tone… 'we can do this' optimism", yet "every play will… be a narrative of all-too-relatable crisis and disaster". It is "beautifully crafted and actually huge fun". — [Tabletop Gaming](https://tabletopgaming.co.uk/reviews/daybreak-game-review)
- It is used for climate learning ([Nevada Humanities, 2025](https://www.nevadahumanities.org/blog/2025/3/3/playing-with-science-learning-about-climate-change-through-the-board-game-daybreak)). A second Opinionated Gamers review is titled "Review of Daybreak (eMission)" (July 2024) ([Opinionated Gamers](https://opinionatedgamers.com/2024/07/15/dale-yu-review-of-daybreak-emission/)).

**Keep Cool (2004), Klaus Eisenack and Gerhard Petschel-Held (Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, PIK)**
- Published by Spieltrieb in November 2004. 3–6 players aged 12+ represent groups of countries (such as Europe, OPEC, developing countries). For one to two hours they choose between "black" and "green" growth and adapt to unavoidable climate impacts such as droughts and floods, through negotiation. — [Wikipedia: Keep Cool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Cool_(board_game))
- It sold out within four months, which led to a second edition. — [PIK news](https://www.pik-potsdam.de/@@multilingual-selector/2c51933b7fa247189e44eab6f5e4dc1f/en)
- It has been used by NGOs, schools, universities and gaming conventions. The research behind it includes Eisenack, "Interdisciplinary communication and education with a board game on climate change", *Simulation & Gaming* (2012), and Reckien & Eisenack, "Climate change gaming on board and screen: a review", *Simulation & Gaming* (2013). — [Wikipedia: Keep Cool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keep_Cool_(board_game)); [HU Berlin Resource Economics](https://www.agrar.hu-berlin.de/@@multilingual-selector/90e52d8f65174402a49edcdbf7743f2b/en)

**Barrage (2019, Cranio Creations)**
- Set in an alternative 1930s where fossil fuels are depleted. Players run competing hydroelectric companies in the Alps, combining worker placement, resource management and network building. They build dams, conduits and powerhouses. — [Frontline Gaming review (2025)](https://frontlinegaming.org/2025/05/21/board-game-review-barrage/); [Board Game Quest](https://www.boardgamequest.com/barrage-review/)
- **Construction wheel.** Committed resources and building tiles "are only recovered after a full rotation", which adds a layer of planning around resource timing. — [Board Game Quest](https://www.boardgamequest.com/barrage-review/)
- **Water.** Dams block water from reaching opponents downstream. "Water is never consumed—its flow is just used to produce energy—it is instead released back to the rivers". (The same extract says conduits "can only be used by their owner"; see Gaps.)

**GigaWatt (2022), Wouter Vink and Jöbke Janssen**
- Six European regions compete to be the first to become independent of coal and gas while still meeting demand. Its 1–6 players "have to deal with the variability of sun and wind, protests from residents, and local differences". — [TU Delft *Delta*](https://delta.tudelft.nl/?p=58070); [Dice Tower entry](https://dicetower.com/board-game/333481)

**Household and prosumer games**
- *Watts the Deal?* (UCL Energy Institute, PACE group) is a peer-to-peer energy-trading board game. Players are households that "have to meet their daily electricity demand exactly using power from solar panels, batteries, and trading with other players and the grid". — [CREDS](https://www.creds.ac.uk/energy-trading-board-game-online-and-easy-to-print/)

**Brass (Lancashire 2007; Birmingham), Martin Wallace et al., as an industrial-economy benchmark**
- *Brass: Birmingham* is the sequel to Wallace's 2007 *Brass*. It covers Birmingham from 1770 to 1870, with industries and with canal and rail networks. Coal and iron "can now go up to 8 per cube" (the extract shows "$"; the game's currency is £). Goods are sold to traders at the board edge, and selling consumes beer (for example, a level-1 cotton mill needs one beer to flip). — [Meeple Mountain](https://www.meeplemountain.com/boardgame/brass-birmingham/); [boardgameshop.nl](https://boardgameshop.nl/en/product/brass-birmingham/)

**18xx (railway share-market family)**
- Trains "eventually become obsolete and must be replaced by more-expensive trains that also have greater capacity for earning revenue". Older trains "rust" and leave the game as newer ones arrive. - Share prices respond to player actions and company performance. The skill lies in "managing obsolescence… forcing reinvestment and strategic timing on when to cash out". — [Wikipedia: 18XX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX); [Gamicus: 18XX series](https://gamicus.gamepedia.com/18XX_(series))

### Inferences
**How each game models the four things Gridmaster cares about**

- **Dice are tabletop shorthand for intermittency** (Energy Empire, GigaWatt). Power Grid ignores intermittency altogether. A video game can replace dice with weather years modelled on historical data, which is more authentic and more learnable, but it must show the distribution so variance feels fair.
- **CO2's permit market is the tabletop precedent for an emissions-trading carbon price.** In Gridmaster, a carbon price should raise fossil marginal costs. That lifts the clearing price, which supports renewables' revenue. The result is "policy as a market variable" that the player can read, not an arbitrary subsidy.
- **Shared-loss thresholds (CO2's 500 ppm, Daybreak's temperature) need co-op or semi-co-op tables.** In a single-player tycoon they work better as macro pressure: a carbon price, policy ambition and climate-linked weather events that respond to how far the whole sector has progressed.
- **Barrage shows that hydro is about flows and timing,** not just capacity. River cascades, upstream operators' releases, reservoirs that store energy as water, and drought years can make Gridmaster's hydro feel distinct from wind and solar. The construction wheel is a strong metaphor for long lead times on wind, offshore and nuclear projects, with capital tied up until commissioning.
- **GigaWatt validates the European, multi-region framing,** with resident protests standing in for permitting friction and accelerating demand standing in for electrification. Its designers' explicit aim of creating enthusiasm, and Daybreak's "we can do this" tone, both argue for an agentic, optimistic framing of the transition rather than doom.
- **Watts the Deal? is a natural model for Gridmaster's "a few solar panels" opening.** A self-contained household-scale puzzle (match demand with PV, a battery and trading) can teach load profiles and storage before market scale arrives.
- **Brass, ranked #1 on BGG, and 18xx show that strategy players love interdependent economies and obsolescence they can plan around.** Telegraphed rusting turns technological change into timing skill rather than punishment.
- **Whitespace:** none of the sourced games turns renewable price cannibalisation, negative prices or storage arbitrage into a mechanic. This is open design ground for Gridmaster.

- **"Power Plants"** (and other titles from BGG's energy categories, such as *Electropolis*): could not be searched before the search budget ran out. BGG's category pages were blocked.
- BGG ratings and ranks for CO2, Second Chance, Energy Empire, Daybreak, Barrage, GigaWatt and Keep Cool: not retrieved.

  - CO2 uses a project pipeline (proposal → installation → construction) in which other players can act on a proposed project.
  - In *Brass*, selling to the coal/iron market fills the highest-priced empty space first and buying takes the cheapest. Using an opponent's coal, iron or beer helps that opponent flip (score) their tiles, a positive externality. Play runs in two eras (canal, then rail).
  - 18xx share prices rise on dividends and fall on withheld dividends and share sales.

## 3. Serious and educational games and interactive simulations: how they make merit order, scarcity, cannibalisation, negative prices and arbitrage understandable, and what was fun or dull

### Takeaway
Serious energy games teach mainly through three approaches:
- **Role-play inside a market:** classroom electricity-market games where student teams act as generating companies bidding into a uniform-price exchange, and Power TAC's software-agent brokers.
- **Immediate what-if feedback:** En-ROADS' sliders inside the Climate Action Simulation role-play.
- **Simple build-and-balance loops:** EnerCities.

Evaluations report gains in knowledge, motivation and empowerment, and very positive student feedback. Fate of the World shows the failure mode: realism that is "frustratingly badly explained". I found no serious game that explicitly teaches renewable price cannibalisation, negative prices or storage arbitrage, even though the real-market mechanisms are well documented. That is a clear gap Gridmaster could fill.

### Cited Findings

**Fate of the World (2011, Red Redemption)**
- The player heads a global environmental organisation charged with saving the world over the next 200 years, facing rising temperatures, diminishing resources, disappearing ecosystems and growing population. Policies include cap-and-trade, promotion of renewables and geoengineering. — [Wikipedia: Fate of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fate_of_the_World)

**Real-market mechanisms a game would need to convey** (sourced, for calibrating mechanics)
- Renewables have "virtually zero marginal costs" and "gradually displac[e] more expensive power plants in the merit order". — [Kyon Energy glossary: Merit Order](https://www.kyon-energy.de/en/glossar/merit-order)
- More variable renewable energy leads to more negative prices. Negative prices come from "large volumes of zero-price offers from renewable suppliers and thermal producers willing to incur occasional losses to avoid wear-and-tear of their units". — [ScienceDirect, *Energy Policy* (2022), "The impact of renewables on the incidents of negative prices in the energy spot markets"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421522002981)
- Storage arbitrage means buying low to charge and selling high. "More frequent negative prices allow for more profitable arbitrage by efficient energy storage." — [Flex Power, "BESS on energy markets"](https://flex-power.energy/school-of-flex/bess-energy-markets/); [ScienceDirect, "Maximising the value of electricity storage"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352152X1630113X)
- Recent literature on the PV "duck curve", merit-order dynamics and batteries setting imbalance prices: [MDPI *Sustainability* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.3390/su17104618); [arXiv 2608.29818 (GB Balancing Mechanism)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.29818)

### Inferences
- **Merit order is best learned by being a plant.** Every classroom market game puts players on the supply side, bidding and watching the uniform clearing price pay inframarginal plants more than their bids. For Gridmaster this argues for occasional direct bidding decisions (curtail or not at negative prices; set storage strategies), not only passive price-taking. A fully automated market hides the lesson.
- **Immediate counterfactual feedback is the engagement engine.** En-ROADS' evaluated gains come from moving a lever and instantly seeing the system respond. Gridmaster should give a preview before every major build: the projected capture price, the effect on the zone's price curve and the payback range.
- **Explanation quality decides whether realism is fun or frustrating.** Fate of the World's accuracy was not enough: "badly explained" is the failure mode to design against. Every price change and penalty should answer "why?" in one click.
- **What players found fun:** competition and role-play (classroom games), agency and empowerment (En-ROADS), and building something visible (EnerCities). **What frustrated them:** opacity and unrelenting difficulty (Fate of the World). There was little evidence about "dull" specifically.
- **Power TAC shows the complete retail-market complexity** (tariffs, balancing penalties, weather-driven renewables, stochastic customers) is fully specifiable, but it is meant for software agents. A human-facing game must abstract it. Its broker logic is a reference for AI competitors.
- **Natural difficulty arc:** the documented chain runs from more variable renewables, to more negative prices, to more profitable storage arbitrage. This is exactly Gridmaster's intended progression from solar to saturation to batteries, so the real economics already supply the arc.
- **Gap = opportunity:** no accessible game was found that makes cannibalisation, negative prices or arbitrage legible. Gridmaster can be the first, but only if it explains well (the Fate of the World lesson).

### Gaps
- **Energy Transition Model (Quintel):** no source found describing it as a game or reporting any evaluation. Searches returned other energy-transition games instead.
- **Danish and Nordic serious energy games:** none found. The extended search returned only DTU and Energinet programme pages and the Norwegian UiO school workshops. NTNU and Nord Pool market games could not be searched (budget exhausted).
- **Unverified** (background knowledge only, check before use):
  - Severin Borenstein's "Electricity Strategy Game" at UC Berkeley: teams own generation portfolios and bid into a uniform-price market, illustrating market power. It is widely used in energy-economics courses.
  - EnerCities was funded under the EU "Intelligent Energy Europe" programme and scores a balance of People / Planet / Profit.
  - Fate of the World uses region-based policy cards in 5-year turns.
  - The FT "Climate Game" (2022) is a widely played net-zero web simulation.
- **No quantitative evaluation data** were retrieved: effect sizes, sample sizes, Metacritic scores. None of the journals were reachable.
- I found no evaluation that specifically compares how understandable scarcity pricing, cannibalisation or arbitrage are in games.

## 4. Synthesis: transferable design patterns for Gridmaster (single-player, possibly real-time with pause)

### Takeaway
The games above converge on a small set of patterns that fit Gridmaster's pitch:
- a price that the player's own actions move, shown visibly;
- a window onto future opportunities;
- negative feedback that has a real-world reason;
- auctions that punctuate the flow of play;
- technology that arrives on a historical timetable and goes obsolete on a schedule the player can see coming;
- phase changes triggered by progress;
- variance the player can hedge;
- geography as rule modules;
- rivals with readable, modular personalities.

The central problem, "the price falls when everyone builds solar", becomes a fun strategic tension when it is visible, foreseeable and counterable, and when other assets profit from it (storage, flexible demand, other regions). It frustrates when it is invisible, sudden or unavoidable.

### Cited Findings
- Power Grid prices fuel on a ladder that every player drains, refilled from tables that vary by Step, player count and map. — [BGG "How To Teach"](https://boardgamegeek.com/wiki/page/How_To_Teach:_Power_Grid); [Rio Grande Québec rules](https://www.riograndegames.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Power-Grid-Quebec-Rules.pdf)
- Its catch-up (weakest goes first, diminishing income) curbs runaway leaders but is criticised as "gamey" and rewards sandbagging. — [There Will Be Games](https://therewillbe.games/articles-boardgame-reviews/1874-power-grid-a-calculated-assessment); [Zatu](https://zatu.com/en-au/blogs/reviews/power-grid-second-opinion-where-calculators-are-the-real-power-plants)
- Progress-triggered Step 2 (7 cities) and an end trigger (17 cities with 3–4 players). — [edp.org rules](https://edp.org/Games/PowerGrid.pdf); [UltraBoardGames](https://www.ultraboardgames.com/power-grid/beginners-guide.php)
- The Robots' modular behaviour tiles give tabletop AI opponents. — [Board Game Bliss](https://www.boardgamebliss.com/products/power-grid-the-robots-recharged)
- 18xx's phase change on the first purchase of a new train type, and rusting. — [Wikipedia: 18XX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18XX)
- Barrage's shared, reusable water flow and its capital-locking construction wheel. — [Board Game Quest](https://www.boardgamequest.com/barrage-review/)
- GigaWatt's sun and wind dice, accelerating demand and protests across European regions. — [Tabletopia rules](https://c.tabletopia.com/games/gigawatt-2022/rules/20211208-gigawatt-rules-en/en); [TU Delft *Delta*](https://delta.tudelft.nl/?p=58070)
- En-ROADS' interactive feedback produced knowledge gains and a sense of empowerment. Fate of the World's poor explanation hurt reception. — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/136266); [Wikipedia: Fate of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fate_of_the_World)
- Negative prices rise with the share of variable renewables, and storage arbitrage becomes more profitable as negative prices become more frequent. — [ScienceDirect *Energy Policy* 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421522002981); [Flex Power](https://flex-power.energy/school-of-flex/bess-energy-markets/)

### Inferences
**P1. Make the merit-order ladder a first-class screen (Power Grid's resource market, inverted).**
- *Precedent and why it works:* a shared market whose state everyone changes. Each action has a visible externality, so reading and timing the market becomes the skill.
- *Gridmaster:*
  - For each bidding zone and hour, show a supply stack against a demand line. Renewables sit near €0. Nuclear, lignite, coal, gas, peakers and imports sit above them, at heights set by fuel and carbon prices.
  - Highlight the player's own blocks.
  - Show the effect of each new build, for example "your 40 MW moves the noon price from €48 to €31".
- Power Grid's refill table maps onto era-dependent fuel, carbon and demand inputs drawn from historical-data-inspired series. Its map-specific supply tables map onto country profiles.
- *Pitfall:* a chart is not a game. The player must be able to act on the ladder through timing, location, technology mix, storage and contracts.

**P2. Ten levers that make "the price falls when everyone builds solar" a strategic tension instead of a frustration.**
1. **Legibility.** Show capture price and capture rate per technology and per zone, and break each asset's profit and loss into volume × capture price − curtailment − balancing.
2. **Foresight.** Provide a Power Grid-style future-market row: rivals' pipelines, the grid queue, upcoming tenders and the technology cost outlook, 1–3 years ahead. Saturation should never ambush the player.
3. **Counter-assets that profit from the problem.** Batteries, pumped hydro and reservoir hydro buy the dips; real arbitrage value rises as negative prices become more frequent. Power-to-X electrolysers (which turn electricity into hydrogen or fuels) get cheaper input as solar saturates. Flexible-demand contracts work the same way. Owning both sides turns rivals' overbuilding into the player's opportunity.
4. **Diversification with real correlations.** Wind and solar have different profiles, north and south Europe differ, and interconnectors link them. This echoes Power Grid's "everyone on coal drives coal up, so switch fuel".
5. **First-mover windows.** Early entrants to a zone earn high capture prices. Reading saturation and moving to another market is the game's stated risk-spreading fantasy.
6. **Hedges.** Fixed-price contracts with buyers (PPAs) and auctioned price-guarantee contracts (CfD-style rounds) trade upside for certainty.
7. **Operating choices.** Curtailing at negative prices, storage dispatch policy and simple bidding stances. The classroom games show that bidding is where the learning happens.
8. **Historical support regimes as a difficulty ramp.** Price-insulated fixed tariffs early on give way to market exposure later. The specific dates and schemes are to be verified by the market-history research.
9. **Rivals suffer too.** Overbuilt AI rivals produce distressed sales and buyouts, which rewards patience without rewarding sandbagging.
10. **Score delivered value.** Use Power Grid's "cities powered" principle, and warn before a build whose projected value is negative, so the player does not walk into trap builds.

**P3. Prefer negative feedback with a real-world reason to "gamey" catch-up.**
- Get the anti-snowball effect from realistic sources:
  - merit-order cannibalisation;
  - depletion of the best sites;
  - connection costs that rise with distance (Power Grid's map) and later, pricier slots (its 10/15/20 city slots, unverified figures);
  - grid congestion and curtailment;
  - permitting friction (GigaWatt's protests);
  - capital lock-up (Barrage's wheel);
  - risk from concentrating in one weather pattern or zone.
- Balance these with positive loops: learning-curve cost declines, reputation that eases permitting, and portfolio synergies.
- Never make underperformance optimal.

**P4. Auctions as punctuation, with lots shown in advance and readable rivals.**
- Scheduled tenders (offshore sites, grid capacity, CfD-like rounds) pause real time.
- Each lot has a reserve price, as Power Grid's minimum bid equals the plant number.
- The next lots are shown, mirroring Power Grid's current and future rows.
- Wins per round are capped, as Power Grid's one plant per player per round forces prioritisation.
- Over-bidding carries winner's-curse risk.
- Rivals are built from modular behaviour profiles, following The Robots' five tiles: opening siting, bidding, input purchasing, expansion and a special ability. Archetypes might include an incumbent utility, an oil major pivoting to renewables, an infrastructure fund and a citizens' cooperative.

**P5. Technology progression: historical gates, learning curves and obsolescence on a known schedule.**
- Technologies arrive in historical windows: solar, onshore wind, batteries, offshore wind, then power-to-X and experimental technologies.
- Their costs and their effects on the game respond to deployment.
- The first adoption in a zone triggers a phase, following 18xx's "first new train" rule. For example, the first grid-scale battery could open an ancillary-services market.
- Obsolescence is announced ahead: support-scheme expiry, PV degradation, ageing assets. 18xx's rusting is fun because it is known in advance.
- Experimental technologies can mirror Power Grid's high-numbered plants: expensive, efficient, scarce, and uncertain because of R&D risk.

**P6. Steps triggered by progress, plus scenario end triggers.**
- Combine the historical calendar with thresholds. For example, once variable renewables pass a share threshold in a zone, negative-price hours begin. Once price spreads pass a threshold, storage becomes bankable.
- Possible end conditions:
  - deadline goals, such as "decarbonise Denmark's power by 2035";
  - races against AI rivals, in the style of Power Grid's "first to N cities";
  - an open sandbox with a legacy score covering profit, delivered MWh, CO2 avoided and reliability.
- Consider a disruptive event whose timing is uncertain within a known window, as with the Step 3 card: a fuel-price crisis or a breakthrough technology.

**P7. Offload arithmetic, keep judgement.**
- Automate settlement and show previews: payback, capture price after the build, and expected-versus-bad-year yields (P50/P90).
- Keep the uncertainty in weather years, rival behaviour and policy.
- Layer the interface: summary indicators for casual play, drill-down data for experts, matching "casual but realistic".
- Power Grid's calculators and analysis paralysis are the cautionary tale.

**P8. Variance players can hedge (from dice to weather years).**
- Replace Energy Empire's and GigaWatt's dice with sampled weather years modelled on history.
- Show yield distributions, and offer insurance through diversification, storage and contracts.
- Windless, sunless weeks, droughts and scarcity-price spikes are set pieces where flexible assets pay off. They dramatise "batteries arrive as volatility rises".

**P9. Geography as rule modules.** Power Grid's European maps change resources and politics; GigaWatt's regions have "local differences". In Gridmaster each country or zone would be a module with:
- resource quality (wind, sun, hydro);
- demand growth;
- a policy timeline;
- public acceptance;
- grid strength and interconnectors.

This adds replayability and the risk-spreading fantasy, and Power Grid's 16+ maps prove a map-pack model.

**P10. Emissions as macro pressure, not a fail state.**
- Use a carbon price (CO2's CEP market is the precedent) and policy ambition that respond to progress across the sector, plus climate-linked events.
- Avoid single-player shared-loss endings.
- Keep the tone agentic and optimistic, as in Daybreak, GigaWatt and the En-ROADS "empowered" finding.

**P11. Shared flows and infrastructure.** Following Barrage's upstream/downstream water and Brass's shared networks:
- river cascades in which upstream operators' releases matter;
- reservoirs as energy storage;
- shared interconnector and substation capacity, with congestion as a contested resource.

**P12. Explain the model and link it to reality.**
- Give every price movement and penalty a one-click explanation, avoiding Fate of the World's failure.
- Add a codex linking mechanics to real-world explainers and data, as Daybreak does with QR codes.
- Offer En-ROADS-style what-if sliders for planning.

**P13. Start as a prosumer puzzle.** Watts the Deal?'s "meet daily demand exactly" with PV, a battery and trading is a ready tutorial format for the "few panels" opening, before scaling up to market participation.

**P14. Mapping Power Grid's phases to real time with pause.**
- Run dispatch continuously and automatically in merit order, at accelerated time.
- Turn the Bureaucracy phase into periodic settlement: monthly or quarterly accounts, with fuel and carbon updates as the "refill".
- Hold auctions as scheduled pausing events.
- Do planning while paused, with the forecast window open.
- Auto-pause on alerts: a run of negative prices, a scarcity event, a tender opening, a grid-queue decision.

**Pitfalls to avoid** (each seen in the sources):
- optimal sandbagging (Power Grid);
- arithmetic as the main challenge (Power Grid);
- opaque realism (Fate of the World);
- low interaction leaving engine-building feeling solitary (Daybreak; in single-player this means the world must react visibly to the player);
- a direct port of a board-game interface (the 2.5★ app).
